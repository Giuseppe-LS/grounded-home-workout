Food & recovery

Evidence-based general guidance for strength, energy and wellbeing — not a meal plan or restrictive diet.

Your reference

Female • ~160 cm • ~58 kg • beginner • 3 moderate home strength sessions/week

The app does not set a weight-loss target. Food should support training, recovery and general health. Individual needs vary with age, activity, preferences and health history.

The foundations

Variety firstBuild most meals from a mix of vegetables, fruit, whole grains, pulses and varied protein sources.
Fruit & vegetablesA useful population-health reference is at least ~400 g/day for adults.
Fibre-rich carbsWhole grains, potatoes, fruit and pulses are useful fuel; carbohydrates do not need to be avoided.
FatsPrefer mostly unsaturated sources such as olive/rapeseed oil, nuts, seeds and fish.
ProteinInclude a protein source regularly across the day; food is enough for most people.
ModerationFoods high in free sugars, salt and saturated/trans fats can be enjoyed less often rather than labelled “bad”.

Protein & strength

Exercise research commonly uses about 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day for healthy people training regularly. At 58 kg that is roughly 81–116 g/day, but this is an exercise-research range, not a daily requirement or target for this app. For a beginner doing moderate sessions, the practical priority is simply including protein-rich foods across normal meals and eating enough overall.

Examples: yogurt/skyr, milk, eggs, fish, poultry, tofu/tempeh, beans, lentils and other pulses. Protein supplements are optional convenience foods, not necessary by default.

Easy plate idea

For many main meals, think roughly: ½ vegetables/fruit · ¼ protein-rich food · ¼ potatoes/rice/pasta/bread/other grains, with an appropriate fat source.

This is only a visual planning tool. Hunger, meal type, training, culture and preferences can change the proportions.

Training days

There is no need for complicated workout nutrition for a 30-minute home session. Arrive reasonably fed and hydrated. A normal meal a few hours beforehand works well; if hungry closer to training, a simple snack such as fruit with yogurt can work. Afterwards, return to normal eating and include some protein and carbohydrate.

Hydration

Drink regularly through the day and respond to thirst. Needs rise with heat, sweating and activity. For these short home workouts, water is generally sufficient; sports drinks are not routinely needed.

Weight is only one signal

Day-to-day scale weight can move with hydration, food, sodium, bowel contents and the menstrual cycle. Judge trends over weeks rather than reacting to individual readings. Workout consistency, improving control, strength, energy and how exercises feel are also meaningful progress.

Sources & methodology

This section prioritizes major public-health guidance and consensus/position papers rather than social-media fitness advice. It intentionally converts research into conservative everyday guidance instead of prescribing calories or a diet.

WHO — Healthy diet (2026): adequacy, balance, moderation and diversity; emphasis on minimally processed foods, whole grains, vegetables, fruit and pulses; ≥400 g fruit/vegetables and ≥25 g naturally occurring fibre/day for people over 10; limits for free sugars, sodium and unhealthy fats.

Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics + Dietitians of Canada + ACSM — Nutrition and Athletic Performance: appropriate nutrition supports performance and recovery; individualized sports-nutrition plans should be handled by a qualified dietitian/nutritionist.

International Society of Sports Nutrition — Protein and Exercise: 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day is a commonly supported range for healthy exercising individuals, with needs affected by training status, energy intake and other factors.

Evidence informs the principles above; it does not mean every sports-performance recommendation is necessary for a beginner doing moderate home exercise.

Scope: General wellness information only, not diagnosis or individualized nutrition therapy. Pregnancy, medical conditions, medication interactions, kidney disease, gastrointestinal conditions, eating-disorder history, allergies/intolerances or specific dietary needs should be discussed with an appropriate healthcare professional or registered dietitian/nutritionist.
